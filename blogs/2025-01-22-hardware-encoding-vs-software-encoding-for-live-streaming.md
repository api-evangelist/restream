But your encoder is pretty essential &#x2014; you can&#x2019;t go live without a way to encode your stream. Let&#x2019;s get better acquainted with the heart of the streaming setup so you can choose the best encoder for your live stream.</p><h2 id="what-is-a-video-encoder">What is a video encoder?</h2><p>An <a href="https://restream.io/learn/what-is/video-encoder/"><u>encoder</u></a> takes raw analog or digital video and compresses it into a suitable format for broadcasting. <strong>You cannot send&#xa0; your camera footage directly to a YouTube live stream</strong> because it has to be encoded first.</p><p>When you use apps to stream, &#x2014; like going live on your Facebook profile &#x2014; the app takes care of the encoding for you. But when you stream from your laptop or desktop computer, you need another solution for encoding. For most live streaming platforms, you need to convert the video signal into the H.264 format, an industry standard for video compression.</p><p>There are two types of encoders you can use to live stream:</p><ul><li><strong>Software encoders</strong>: Encoding programs that you install and run on your computer. Software encoders capture and compress your stream input and deliver it to an end platform like <a href="https://restream.io/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-facebook-live-streaming/"><u>Facebook</u></a>, <a href="https://restream.io/blog/ultimate-guide-to-twitch/"><u>Twitch</u></a> or <a href="https://restream.io/blog/ultimate-guide-to-youtube-live/"><u>YouTube</u></a>.</li><li><strong>Hardware encoders</strong>: Physical equipment designed to encode live video signals. Some, but not all, hardware encoders connect directly to a camera.</li></ul><p>The encoder is like the video translator for your streaming setup, which is why it&#x2019;s important. Take it out of the equation and video streaming platforms would have a hard time understanding and reproducing the data you&#x2019;re sending them.</p><h2 id="hardware-vs-software-encoding">Hardware vs. software encoding</h2><p>So which is better, a software encoder or a hardware encoder? An encoder includes&#xa0; an algorithm and the hardware needed to run it too. In some sense, hardware encoders are very similar to their software counterparts: By the time you&#x2019;re using them in your setup, they&#x2019;ll both be just algorithms and hardware. But that&#x2019;s the end of their similarities. They&#x2019;re <strong>different in fundamental ways</strong>, and each comes with its own set of pros and cons.</p><h3 id="what-are-the-pros-and-cons-of-hardware-encoders">What are the pros and cons of hardware encoders?</h3><p>Hardware encoders pack all you need in a neat little box. They have a powerful processing unit whose sole job is to run the aforementioned encoding algorithm. Most hardware encoders have an interface to&#xa0; choose between options. For example, you can <strong>pick the encoding codec or the destination of the stream </strong>on your encoder.</p><p>Here are some benefits of a hardware encoder:</p><ul><li><strong>Great performance</strong>, especially when you need high-quality or UHD streaming.</li><li><strong>Robust reliability</strong>, given that they&#x2019;re built with one purpose &#x2014; to encode.</li><li><strong>Higher encoding speed</strong>, leading to lower latency and less stream delay.</li></ul><p>Hardware encoders do have their downsides, though, including:</p><ul><li><strong>Cost, </strong>which<strong> </strong>starts at a couple hundred dollars and only increases from there.</li><li><strong>Functional limits</strong>, meaning you&#x2019;ll have to get additional gear for other streaming actions like video switching.</li><li><strong>Upgrade difficulties</strong>, especially compared to software encoders. Like we said, hardware encoders do one thing very well, and that&#x2019;s it. The good ones encode incredibly quickly, but at the cost of an arm and a leg. But if you can justify the investment, you probably won&#x2019;t look back. Be ready to make additional investments, though, because <strong>hardware encoders are a one-trick pony.</strong></li></ul><h3 id="what-are-the-pros-and-cons-of-software-encoders">What are the pros and cons of software encoders?</h3><p>Software encoders don&#x2019;t come with their own hardware. <strong>They rely entirely on your computer&#x2019;s CPU for processing power</strong>. That can pose some problems, as encoding software can use up lots of PC resources. Still, many streamers use software encoders for the benefits they offer.</p><p>The key traits that make software encoders so popular are:</p><ul><li><strong>Affordability</strong>, because even top-of-the-line encoding software doesn&#x2019;t cost extra.</li><li><strong>Flexibility</strong>, as they can handle multiple input feeds and act as switchers, provide effects and more.</li><li><strong>Customizability</strong>, as they let you change the bitrate, adjust the encoding type and more.</li></ul><p>It&#x2019;s not all gravy with software encoders, though, because of their:</p><ul><li><strong>High dependence on the host</strong>, since they&#x2019;re only as good as the computer running them.</li><li><strong>Higher latency</strong>, thanks to the slower encoding.</li><li><strong>Overall lower performance</strong>, especially for lower-end setups or while you&#x2019;re multitasking.</li></ul><p>Like their hardware counterparts, software encoders have their flaws. But the good ones are incredibly cost-effective and offer a myriad of creative customization options. Still, a high-end software encoder likely won&#x2019;t beat a high-end hardware encoder when it comes to performance and latency.</p><h2 id="should-i-use-software-or-hardware-encoding-for-my-live-stream">Should I use software or hardware encoding for my live stream?</h2><p>What you want and what you need are two completely different things. You should keep this in mind when buying gear for your stream setup. Your goal is to get the best possible options within your budget &#x2014; and that often means making compromises.</p><p>To start, you should always buy the gear that <strong>meets your needs</strong>. Regular streams usually don&#x2019;t match the quality of a professional broadcast, and that&#x2019;s perfectly fine.</p><p><strong>Affordability is the major factor</strong> that will influence your decision. Hardware encoders are expensive, and you&#x2019;ll need other gear to account for what they can&#x2019;t do on their own. But high-end <a href="https://restream.io/blog/how-to-choose-the-best-streaming-software/"><u>software encoders</u></a> can be expensive too. Often enough, you&#x2019;ll need either a powerful PC or a dedicated streaming computer just to run them.</p><p>Still, a good software encoder and a decent computer goes a long way in the streaming world. Using a software encoder could save you money that could go towards a good <a href="https://restream.io/blog/best-microphones-for-streaming/"><u>microphone</u></a>, <a href="https://restream.io/blog/best-webcams/"><u>camera</u></a>, or better internet connection.</p><p>So unless you&#x2019;re a professional broadcaster or big-time streamer, a software encoder should help you stream successfully for a while. It&#x2019;s cheaper, more accessible, and easier to use &#x2014; a clear winner in most situations.</p><h2 id="popular-software-and-hardware-encoders">Popular software and hardware encoders</h2><p>Here are popular video encoding and streaming software choices among streamers:</p><ul><li><a href="https://restream.io/learn/obs-studio/"><strong><u>OBS Studio</u></strong></a><strong>: </strong>This free, open-source encoding software is a top choice for streamers of all kinds with a resourceful community to help you master OBS.</li><li><strong>XSplit: </strong>Professional streamers of all kinds use XSplit for capturing gameplay, <a href="https://restream.io/blog/how-to-create-a-video-presentation/"><u>streaming presentations</u></a>, and hosting live events.</li><li><strong>vMix: </strong>vMix is a live streaming software for Windows users.
